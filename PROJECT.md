Recipe for programs: give the fact → short think → program → free check → full think only on a visible failure → think again once on failure. Honest
summary: against a baseline that also thinks again on failure, the recipe is the cheap end of a cost–accuracy trade-off, not a free win;
without labels short think first is the default (0.2–1.5 points below each cell's best on five task types; text-to-SQL 0.33), and labels mainly tell a
contest-like task, where one full think should go first, from the rest (PX: 30 labels add about two points of selection noise elsewhere).

## 1. Goal

**Purpose: exactly right answers from LLMs at the lowest cost**, with open models from 8B to 120B. Every result is reported as
accuracy *and* cost: output tokens, with utility = 100 × right − 0.5 × thousand output tokens unless a task states its own price.
Compute: mainly the RTX 3090 partition (24 GB each; larger models run tensor-parallel over several 3090s in one job), so as not to
compete with other users (user, 2026-10-23: no longer limited to 24 GB models).

**Problems are open, not fixed** (user, 2026-10-30 and 2026-11-05: the goal must not narrow the project to computational problems or to
the methods we have). The project covers any task where a model's answer can be checked, selected or made exact by some means:

- computation: geometry, optimization, contest code, text-to-SQL;
- tool calls and multi-turn agents;
- instruction following;
- proofs (Lean, natural language, geometry through a symbolic engine);
- constructions.

A new problem type enters with a related-work check and a pre-registration.

**Questions (examples, not a boundary):**

- Where should a fixed budget go (thinking, sampling, search, a second model, tools) for each problem and model?
- What can tell a right answer from a wrong one? Options are a free check, agreement with another model, a verifier trained with
  labels used only in training, or a translation into a formal system whose engine is exact. How should the choice among candidates
  be made?
- What should be trained or built when prompting and checks run out? Candidates are quick answers trained for the cascade,
  verifiers, translators, auxiliary-point search, and generated environments.
- When does a method pay? We use theory here: bounds, how per-step gains compound over a conversation, and derived decision rules.

**Methods are open too.** What follows is what we have established so far, not the scope. The free-check recipe is for programs.
Other methods so far: trained verifiers choosing among quick, peer and thinking answers (SQL, tool calls, and at every turn of an
agent loop: MT-SO), and translation into a formal language with a symbolic prover (SIDE-GT).

**The recipe established for programs:** write a program → add the missing fact (a few lines of exact library usage, or the model's own checked program for
a similar problem, found with no labels) → run a free check (the program runs; its outputs on moved / rotated / mirrored copies agree; the
statement's examples pass) → when the check visibly fails, one cheap repair turn with the error (word problems), then think harder →
reuse a checked program when the problem repeats. For models whose free check leaks (their passing non-thinking programs are often
wrong), the first program is written after a short think (2,000 tokens, budget forcing; 4,000 only for Nemotron-3-Nano / Qwen3-8B, HO-SB4, and not on three other models, HO-4K; 1k is too short; fixed, not a pilot choice, HO-PB; gpt-oss: medium effort cut at 2k
instead of low effort, HO-OSB) instead of none (HO-SB); the check applies to
every step, so a visibly failed think is retried (HO-CM).

## 2. Main claim and where it stands

**Where it stands, 2026-11-01 (one paragraph; details below).** On tasks whose answer can be executed (text-to-SQL, tool calls), the
cheapest exact answers come from the quick answer, its free check, and a second model's quick answer: keep the quick answer when it
passes and the other model returns the same result, else run the default (short think → think → retry). It beats that default, more
accurate and cheaper, for Qwen and gpt-oss generators (BIRD, BFCL; XG), and on a benchmark never used for any decision (Spider test,
4 of 5 tests under Holm; FINAL), and a quick query trained for correctness on BIRD makes it larger there (+3.7) and on Spider dev, a
second untouched benchmark (+2.5; TR-C1). It beats the standard
one-model alternative, the answer's own token confidence, and agreement without the check (EXT). Two analyses bound it: a Bayes-optimal
policy that knows the joint law of the five calls gains at most about a point over the cascade on SQL (B), so the cascade is at the
information limit of its calls there, and more models' quick answers do not lift that limit (B2) — a new signal does: a verifier
trained on BIRD-train labels choosing among the disagreeing passing answers adds +0.9 to +2.0 (SEL, below); and with no labels, unanimous
agreement cannot be told from a shared mistake (LCM), which is why the second model must come from elsewhere and why a fitted policy
needs labels. A derived policy gains where the fixed rule fits the model badly: with a weak peer (Qwen3.5-9B's tool calls, +3.2 /
+4.5). On the hard word problems the earlier "up to +10" was which model of the pair answers, and it did not reappear on two fresh sets
(LS, 11-01); labels from other sets of the same task did about as well as the target's own there, but a policy fitted on BIRD and
Spider test does not beat the cascade on Spider dev, never used (TR, 0 of 4; 11-01): for untrained generators on SQL a derived policy
adds nothing reliable, and "labels once per task" is not supported. Training the quick answer changes this: where the peer disagrees,
an untrained quick answer is far less accurate than the thinks, a trained one about as accurate (SQL, Spider dev: 34% untrained,
47% trained, the thinks 49%; tool calls: 31%, 41%, the thinks 42–45%), so the trained one is worth a vote rather than replacement, and a policy fitted once on the task's public
labelled benchmarks beats the cascade on data never used for the question, on SQL (Spider dev, +0.99 [+0.23, +1.83]; TR-C1) and on
tool calls (fitted on BFCL, 3,549 held-out ToolACE requests, +0.54 [+0.14, +0.96]; TRT, 11-01), at 1.7–1.9× the cascade's tokens;
with untrained quick answers it does not (TRT base −0.29). Fitted instead on held-out questions of the quick answer's own training
split (TL, 11-01), it carries on tool calls (BFCL +1.04 [+0.60, +1.49], as much as BFCL's own labels give; base −0.11) but not on
SQL (BIRD dev −0.43, Spider test +0.39, Spider dev +0.39, none significant): on SQL one fit / target pair in five is significant for
the trained quick query, and the small room above the cascade belongs to the target set (Spider test: +0.7 / +0.8 with its own labels
for trained and base alike; BIRD dev none), not to training. With Qwen3-4B answering (TL4, 11-01) the fit on held-out training requests

never significantly worse), with a trained verifier choosing at its stop (SEL-TC, SEL-TC2, and with "no call" among its choices SEL-TC2-AB, its weight fitted for the choice it makes, SEL-CL: 88.3% / 88.3% at 0.43k / 0.48k tokens vs the

## 4. Working rules

1. **Pre-register.** Before a run, write the predictions with numbers and a decision rule in `docs/prereg/PREREG_<id>.md`; append results
   below, never edit the predictions.
2. **Only runs that can change the main claim.** Ask first: could this result change what we claim? If not, skip it — no replications or
   baselines for their own sake.
   **Do not focus on easy or incremental things** (user, 2026-10-24, 10-29, 11-01 and 11-05; "are you doing easy or incremental
   things?"). Lead with the hard, risky method that could move the cost–accuracy frontier, build it, and report it even if it fails.
   - These do not count as the work, and may only serve a method track:
     - offline re-analyses, audits and "measure X first" stages;
     - one more task type, model, seed set or fresh-instance confirmation;
     - budget reruns, swapping in a bigger model, and post-hoc knob variants (then registering the best one);
     - plumbing and API fixes;
     - re-implementing a published baseline;
     - write-ups.
   - At every check, first name each active track's hardest open gap, then run only what attacks it. When a planned run turns out to be
     incremental, withdraw it, as SIDE-MC-H2 was (PREREG_SIDE).
     **Theory counts as evidence** (user, 2026-10-29): use mathematical / theoretical analysis where it helps, not only empirical tests —
     derive what a method can reach (bounds, decompositions, optimal policies) before or alongside running it, and let the derivation
     shape the design and the pre-registered predictions.
     **No heuristics** (user, 2026-11-01: "don't do easy or incremental things, and don't suggest heuristics"): no hand-set thresholds,
     fixed orders, clamps or "at least N" cut-offs as a method or a recommendation. A decision rule is derived from an explicit model
     (Bayes-optimal or decision-theoretic), its parameters are estimated, and its assumptions are stated and tested.
3. **At least 3 seeds** for any positive claim; report seed means and, for comparisons, a 95% interval over problems; call a tie a tie.
4. **Accuracy with its cost:** output tokens per problem always; input tokens where prompts differ in length.
5. **Read the raw outputs**, not only scores; treat a surprising number as a harness bug until the raw programs say otherwise.
6. **Record:** a dated entry (UTC, from `date -u`) appended to `docs/FINDINGS.md` for every result; `docs/proposal/PROPOSAL.md` and `PROPOSAL_ko.md`
   updated together, short, numbers edited in place. Slides (`docs/proposal/*.pptx`, `poc/make_slides.js`) change only when asked.
   **Paused (user, 2026-11-05: "you don't need to consider proposals for now"):** the proposal files are not updated until the user
   asks again. Results go to FINDINGS, the prereg files, PROJECT and REPORT.
7. **Git:** commit and push after each change. The repository is public: scan the staged diff for secrets, never commit raw outputs
   (`*.jsonl`, logs), server addresses or keys; no co-author lines — commits are by the team's account.
8. **Explain for a first-time reader:** what ran, how, the result with its cost, what is next.

**Main track: agent loops (BFCL v4 multi-turn, 600 items, `docs/prereg/PREREG_MT.md`).**

- **Done:**
  - **MT-SO.** At every turn, a trained verifier chooses among the quick turn, the peer model's quick turn and the thinking turn,
    run in sandboxes. It beats thinking at every step by +17.2 / +15.5 utility (Qwen3-8B / Qwen3.5-9B; 48.8% vs 30.8%, 53.9% vs
    38.0%). Every fixed rule loses to thinking at every step.
  - **Why the gain is large (derived).** A conversation is right only when every turn is right, so per-turn gains of +6 to +10
    multiply over 4–5 turns. The model has no free parameters, and predicts the online gain within 2.4 points.
- **Running:**
  - **MT-SO-ENV.** The verifier is trained on 4 of BFCL's 8 environment classes and tested online on the other 4. Offline P4 holds
    (+4.03 on later turns). The compounding model's prediction (+9.7 / +10.4 accuracy) was registered before any online row was
    read.
  - **MT-PW.** The hardest open gap: within each turn's set the verifier picks a wrong rollout 11% of the time, and that compounds to
    11 points of conversation accuracy. Half of those errors act where the right rollout asks or abstains. A pairwise verifier that
    sees the alternatives is training; it is decided offline on per-turn choice accuracy, then online.
- **Dropped by derivation:** a gate that skips the thinking rollout. Its ceiling is +3.9 / +1.4 utility, a third of the verifier's.
